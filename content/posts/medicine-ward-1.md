## The admission

Last week, on a calm Friday evening at around 8 PM, I noticed a sting mark on my foot.

The bleeding did not stop for almost 30 minutes, so I asked my friend to take me to the ER.

My childhood friend, who is now a doctor, ran some tests, and by midnight, it was concluded that it might be a snake bite.

I was admitted to Medicine General Male Ward 1.

My sister, my mother, cousins, everyone rushed to the hospital. By 3 AM, my mother was in the room with 2 cousins and 2 friends.

At that point, the threat of death, or of life-altering changes to my body's physiology, was very, very real. Even if they could stop the venom from killing me, it could still leave long-term damage.

Was I scared?

Yes, to some extent.

But I wasn't scared that I would die. I was worried about what would happen to my mother and my sister if I went so soon. It would leave an empty void in their lives that might never be filled.

I was also shit scared about the possibility of surviving, but not having my body in its pristine condition for the rest of my life.

I wasn't allowed to sleep.

Neurotoxic venom can cause your respiratory system to collapse, and if that happened while I was asleep, no one would know until it was too late.

My friend kept re-running a test called the 20WBCT. When a hemotoxic snake bites you, the venom prevents your blood from clotting. That was what he kept checking for throughout the night.

It was also the very first test that came back positive for a toxin at midnight, and it was still positive.

The blood was not clotting.

My friend kept repeating the test until 6 AM. Sometimes there were partial clots. Sometimes there were none. Multiple attending doctors came to see me, each with different opinions about whether I should be given Anti-Snake Venom (ASV).

My mom had heard about some of the severe downsides of ASV, which made her even more anxious.

At 6, my friend concluded that the test was now negative because there were partial clots. He went off to sleep after being sleep-deprived for a day and a half, which, apparently, is a usual doctor schedule.

My sister was 2 hours away and wanted us to wait until she arrived. So my mom and I went to the hospital entrance to receive her, but also to get some sunlight.

Tough night.

Near the entrance, two guards were trying to move a baby snake away from the main gate.

I looked at the snake.

I didn't really feel much.

But I think I'll be very careful from now on.

I was still a patient, and the doctors' morning rounds were about to begin. My friend had instructed me to get back to my bed before 9 AM.

So my mom waited for my sister, and I headed back to the ward.

## Bed No. 21, the doubts and the reality

During the night, I had been in the doctor's duty room because my friend had access to it. So I hadn't really gone to my assigned bed.

This was the first time I'd gone out to the ward.

A nurse told me they had too many patients and were putting two patients on each bed.

The other patient sharing my bed was a guy in his mid-20s, suffering from a mild fever and about to be discharged.

I sat there and looked around.

95% of the patients were old, probably in their 50s.

One of the patients was surrounded by his family, with a cloth tied around his head. Another man, two beds away from me, looked extremely fragile and was barely breathing.

The old man next to me looked fine. In fact, he gave me some advice about a 'desi' cure for the bite.

One man at the start of the ward was completely alone.

No one visiting him. No one sitting beside him.

I remembered something my father had told me once when I said that I might never get married.

"Every man needs a family when he is old."

Here was one patient with his family, being fed and looked after.

And there was another, completely alone.

Am I going to end up like the man at the start of the hallway?

Then I heard a loud cry.

A woman came into the ward crying. She sat down two beds away.

The fragile-looking man had passed away.

I have seen dead people before. But I had never seen someone die right in front of me.

Maybe the man was already dead before I arrived. But it felt like he had died right in front of me.

I had received the best experience a government hospital could give, largely because of my friends and my family.

And here were these people, dying from severe diseases, without receiving the same priority that I was receiving.

Did I deserve the treatment I was being offered?

Wasn't I supposed to be among these men, on one of these beds?

What had I done to deserve the private ward, the priority treatment, and so many people around me?

What had that man done to deserve being alone?

Maybe it was the sleep deprivation speaking, but I couldn't stop thinking about it.

Then I saw a familiar face walking into the ward.

My sister.

As I hugged her, I cried.

Not because I was afraid.

Not because I had missed her.

But because I asked myself:

"What did I do to deserve her coming running to me?"

I walked out of the hospital on Sunday.

I was given the ASV, and the 20WBCT became negative on consecutive trials.

Quite a story to tell at dinner tables.

---

The experience has stayed with me.

Life is fragile. No matter how careful you are, someday there will be an end.

I have started celebrating life without needing a reason.

Recently, I started wearing my 'special occasion' outfits more often.

It's a small thing, but it's a start.

I hope I can work super hard and build myself into someone who deserves even 50% of what I have in life.
